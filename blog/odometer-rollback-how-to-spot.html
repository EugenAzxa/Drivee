Odometer rollback: how to spot it and what to do

The number on the dash is not proof

Odometer rollback is still a real risk with digital clusters, because the tools to reprogram them are easy to buy. Check the car’s wear against its mileage, line up every recorded reading, and get recourse lined up before you pay, not after. Buyers from private sellers have far fewer protections than buyers from licensed dealers.

Drivee Editorial Updated November 15, 2026 9 min read

Odometer rollback means changing a car’s odometer so it shows fewer kilometres or miles than the car has actually travelled. It inflates the price and hides wear, and it hasn’t gone away just because odometers went digital. Here is how odometer rollback works today, how to spot it on the car and in the paperwork, and what you can do about it in Canada and the United States.

How does digital odometer rollback work?

Older mechanical odometers were turned back by physically spinning the gears, which often left scratches, loose screws or digits that didn’t line up. Modern odometers store mileage electronically, so a rollback is done by reprogramming the instrument cluster or the module that holds the reading. Ontario’s dealer regulator, OMVIC, notes that devices capable of reprogramming odometers are readily available.

A digital rollback can leave nothing visible on the dash. That is why the reliable checks are indirect: wear that doesn’t match the mileage, and recorded readings that don’t add up. On many modern cars, mileage is also stored in other control modules, so a dealer or technician with the right scan tool may find a reading that doesn’t match the cluster.

How can you tell if an odometer has been rolled back?

Start with the car itself. Official guidance from OMVIC and the Government of Alberta points to the same warning signs:

  • Wear that doesn’t fit the mileage. A worn or shiny steering wheel, worn driver’s seat bolsters, tired carpets and floor mats, or worn door strikers on a car that is supposed to be lightly used.
  • Worn pedal pads. Rubber on the brake, gas or clutch pedal worn smooth or through.
  • A pitted windshield. A sand-blasted look from years of highway driving.
  • Worn suspension parts beyond what the mileage would explain.
  • Oil change stickers and service records with dates or readings that don’t match the odometer.
  • Signs of tampering on older cars: misaligned digits, a trip odometer that doesn’t work, missing dash screws or scratches near the cluster.
  • An unusually low reading for the age. Alberta’s guidance flags less than 20,000 km a year (about 12,400 miles) as unusually low and worth a closer look.

Watch the seller too. OMVIC lists meeting in a parking lot instead of at home, discouraging a history report or inspection, a name that doesn’t match the ownership, a price well below market and a demand for cash as common red flags.

Tip: low mileage on its own isn’t proof of anything. Plenty of cars really are driven very little. The red flag is low mileage plus wear, records or a story that don’t fit.

How do you check a car’s mileage records?

Collect every reading you can find, with dates, and put them in order. The mileage should only go up, at a pace that makes sense for the car’s use.

  • Ontario UVIP. The $20 package from ServiceOntario lists each Ontario owner along with an odometer reading. OMVIC notes these readings are unverified, so small differences are normal (the UVIP may show slightly more than the dash because of reporting timing). A large drop is not. Order your own copy, because OMVIC also warns that some sellers have altered a UVIP to remove odometer or lien information.
  • Alberta Vehicle Information Report. Includes the odometer reading if one was provided. Alberta notes that reporting a reading is voluntary, so it may be missing.
  • US title and NMVTIS. The US title carries the odometer disclosure made at each transfer. An NMVTIS report shows the latest reported odometer reading.
  • A full vehicle history report. Pulls readings from registrations, inspections, emissions tests and service visits across provinces and states into one timeline.
  • The car’s own paperwork. Service invoices, safety inspection reports and dealer work orders usually record the mileage. Ask for them.

A later reading that is lower than an earlier one is the clearest sign of a rollback or a replaced cluster. A long stretch where the mileage barely moves while the car keeps being registered and serviced is the second.

Kilometres vs miles on cross-border cars

Cars move between Canada and the US all the time, and unit mix-ups are a common source of false alarms, and real fraud. One mile is about 1.609 km. A car that shows 60,000 miles in US records has about 96,500 km on it.

  • If a US record in miles is read as kilometres, the mileage seems to jump when the car arrives in Canada, which is harmless.
  • If a Canadian reading in kilometres is later entered as miles, or a cluster is swapped to a different unit, the car can look like it has far less on it than it does.

Tip: for any car with a US past, convert every reading into one unit before comparing. Check whether the dash is set to km or miles, and whether the cluster looks original.

What does US law say about odometer rollback?

Federal law, in 49 U.S.C. Chapter 327, makes it illegal to disconnect, reset or alter an odometer intending to change the mileage, and to sell or fit a device that makes an odometer show the wrong mileage. If an odometer is repaired or replaced and can’t keep the same reading, it must be set to zero and a notice attached to the left door frame showing the mileage before the work and the date.

Odometer disclosure. Under federal rules (49 CFR Part 580), a seller must state the mileage in writing when ownership is transferred and certify one of three things: the reading is the actual mileage, the mileage exceeds the odometer’s mechanical limits, or the reading is not the actual mileage and should not be relied on. The disclosure is usually made on the title itself.

The 20-year disclosure rule

NHTSA extended the disclosure exemption from 10 to 20 model years. Vehicles from model year 2010 and older stay under the old 10-year exemption and no longer need a disclosure. Model year 2011 and newer must be disclosed until they are 20 model years old, so a 2011 model becomes exempt in 2031. Vehicles with a gross vehicle weight rating over 16,000 pounds are also exempt. Some states apply their own rules on top.

Recourse. A buyer can sue anyone who violated the federal odometer law with intent to defraud for three times the actual damages or $10,000, whichever is greater, plus costs and a reasonable attorney’s fee if they win. The lawsuit must be filed within two years after the claim arises. NHTSA advises reporting tampering to your state’s motor vehicle enforcement authority and, if a dealer was involved, to the state agency that licenses dealers. NHTSA itself investigates large-scale odometer fraud.

What are the odometer rules in Canada?

In Canada, used car sales are regulated by the provinces, so the protection you get depends mostly on where you bought and who you bought from.

Ontario

  • Registered dealers. If an OMVIC-registered dealer fails to disclose that the odometer doesn’t reflect the vehicle’s true distance travelled, the buyer can cancel the contract within 90 days of delivery. Complaints go to OMVIC.
  • Compensation Fund. When a registered dealer is out of business or won’t resolve a proven loss, OMVIC’s Compensation Fund may pay eligible buyers up to $45,000 per transaction. It doesn’t cover private sales.
  • Private sellers and curbsiders. OMVIC can’t help with a private purchase. The usual remedy is to find the seller and sue. Ontario’s Small Claims Court hears claims up to $50,000, a limit in place since October 1, 2025.

Alberta and British Columbia

The Government of Alberta states that odometer tampering is illegal in Alberta, even on an “as is” sale, and lists the Alberta Motor Vehicle Industry Council (AMVIC) as the body that may investigate an odometer complaint. It also suggests a police report and, if the seller won’t refund you, a court claim. In British Columbia, dealers are regulated by the Vehicle Sales Authority of BC.

Tip: buying from a licensed dealer doesn’t make rollback impossible, but it gives you a regulator and, in Ontario, a fund to fall back on. With a private sale, your protection is the checking you do before you pay.

What should you do if you bought a car with a rolled-back odometer?

  1. Gather the evidence. Every report, the UVIP or title, the bill of sale, the ad (screenshot it before it disappears), messages with the seller and photos of the dash.
  2. Get a written opinion. Ask a dealer for that brand or an independent mechanic to scan the modules and note the wear in writing.
  3. Contact the seller in writing. Ask for the contract to be cancelled, or for the difference in value.
  4. Escalate. In Canada, a dealer purchase goes to the provincial regulator (OMVIC, AMVIC or the VSA). In the US, go to the state motor vehicle agency or the dealer licensing agency.
  5. Consider police and court. If you believe you were deliberately deceived, report it to local police. For money, use small claims or civil court, or in the US a federal odometer claim within the two-year limit.

Act quickly. The Ontario dealer window is 90 days from delivery, and a US federal odometer claim must be filed within two years after it arises.

Common questions

How can you tell if an odometer has been rolled back?

Compare the wear with the mileage and line up every recorded reading. Worn pedals, a shiny steering wheel, worn seats and a pitted windshield on a low-mileage car are warning signs. So is any record where a later odometer reading is lower than an earlier one.

Can a digital odometer be rolled back?

Yes. Digital odometers store mileage electronically, and reprogramming tools are readily available. A digital rollback can leave no visible marks, so history records, service paperwork and a mechanic’s inspection matter more than the dash itself.

Is odometer rollback illegal?

Yes. In the US, federal law prohibits altering an odometer to change the mileage, and victims can sue for three times their damages or $10,000, whichever is greater. In Canada the rules are provincial. Alberta, for example, states that tampering is illegal, and Ontario dealers must disclose when an odometer does not show the true distance.

What can I do if a private seller sold me a car with a rolled-back odometer?

Dealer regulators such as OMVIC do not cover private sales, so the usual remedy is to contact the seller in writing and, if that fails, sue. In Ontario, Small Claims Court hears claims up to $50,000. You can also report deliberate fraud to local police.

Does a vehicle history report show odometer rollback?

It can show the signs. A report lists odometer readings recorded at registrations, inspections and service visits, and a later reading that is lower than an earlier one is a strong warning. It cannot catch a rollback made between two records that were never reported.

Do US sellers still have to disclose mileage on older cars?

For model year 2011 and newer vehicles, yes, until the vehicle is 20 model years old. Model year 2010 and older vehicles remain under the old 10-year exemption and no longer need a federal odometer disclosure.
