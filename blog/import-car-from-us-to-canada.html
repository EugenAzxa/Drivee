How to import a car from the US to Canada: the 2026 step-by-step guide

Check the tariff before you buy, not at the border

Since April 9, 2025, Canada has charged a 25% counter-tariff on passenger vehicles and light trucks from the US, and the relief program is for automakers, not individuals. On a US-built car that can outweigh any saving on the sticker price. Confirm the current rules with the CBSA before you commit.

Drivee Editorial Updated November 15, 2026 10 min read

You can import a car from the US to Canada yourself, without a broker, if you do the steps in the right order: check that Canada accepts the model, file the US export paperwork at least 72 hours ahead, declare the car at the border, then pass a federal inspection and your province's safety check. Here is each step, what it costs, and where people get stuck.

Can you import a car from the US to Canada?

Usually, yes, but not every car. A vehicle under 15 years old has to meet Canadian safety standards, and Transport Canada keeps a List of Vehicles Admissible from the United States through the Registrar of Imported Vehicles (RIV). If the exact make, model and year isn't on the list, or the list says it can't be modified to comply, the car can't come in as a regular import.

Two groups work differently:

  • Vehicles more than 15 years old (and buses built before January 1, 1971) aren't regulated for import under the Motor Vehicle Safety Act. Age is counted from the month and year of manufacture on the label. Transport Canada notes this doesn't guarantee a province will license it.
  • Vehicles with open safety recalls need a recall clearance before they can finish the RIV process. RIV accepts a letter on the manufacturer's letterhead stating there are no outstanding recalls, or a printout from an authorized dealer, not a reseller.

Step 1: Check the car before you buy

Most expensive mistakes happen before the car moves. Before you pay a US seller:

  1. Confirm admissibility on the RIV list for that exact year, make and model.
  2. Check recalls and ask the seller or a dealer for a recall clearance letter.
  3. Check the title brand. US states brand titles as salvage, rebuilt, flood and similar. A brand changes the car's value, and you should ask your province's registration office how it treats that brand before you buy.
  4. Check for a lien. If a lender is on the title, US Customs needs a letter from the lienholder authorizing export.
  5. Note the country of manufacture. The first character of the VIN tells you where it was built: 1, 4 or 5 for the US, 2 for Canada, 3 for Mexico. That drives duty and the surtax.

Tip: a US history report is your best defence against flood cars and rolled-back odometers that get washed through state lines. A clean ad is not a clean title.

Step 2: US export and the 72-hour rule

The US side is where most first-time importers get turned back. Under US Customs and Border Protection (CBP) rules for used vehicles crossing by land, the required documents must be submitted to CBP at least 72 hours before export, and the vehicle must be presented to CBP when it leaves. RIV's own guidance is to send them at least three business days ahead.

What you'll need:

  • the original certificate of title (or a certified copy) plus two complete copies,
  • a bill of sale,
  • a letter from the lienholder or lessor if the title shows one,
  • an ITN (Internal Transaction Number) from an electronic export filing in the US Automated Export System (AES). RIV notes this is usually filed by a US freight forwarder or broker, and the car must be on US soil when the filing is made and throughout the export.

Export offices keep their own hours, and many close on weekends and holidays. Call the specific port before you drive there.

Step 3: Declare it at the Canadian border

At the Canadian side, you tell the Canada Border Services Agency (CBSA) you're importing the car. The CBSA completes the Vehicle Import Form, Form 1, or issues a paperless release by email. Keep it. Your province will ask for it, with the federal stamps, before it registers the car.

You pay the GST, any duty, surtax and excise tax to the CBSA at the border. The RIV fee is paid to the RIV after you cross. RIV says the amount appears at the bottom of the paper Form 1 or in the CBSA release email, and a late-payment notice goes out automatically if it isn't paid online within 10 days of import.

Bring originals

Have the title, bill of sale, recall letter and ITN with you, plus proof of the price you paid. The CBSA calculates duty and tax on the value of the car, converted to Canadian dollars.

Is there duty or a tariff on a US car?

Two separate charges can apply, and people often mix them up.

Customs duty. A vehicle that qualifies as originating under CUSMA (the Canada-United States-Mexico Agreement, called USMCA in the US) generally enters duty-free. A car built outside North America, such as a German- or Japanese-built model bought from a US dealer, pays 6.1%.

The 25% counter-tariff. Since April 9, 2025, Canada has applied a 25% surtax on passenger vehicles and light trucks from the US. According to the federal government's April 2026 notice:

  • for a US vehicle that is not CUSMA-compliant, the surtax is charged on the full value for duty, on top of any regular duty,
  • for a CUSMA-compliant US vehicle, the share of value made up of Canadian and Mexican parts is excluded, and that content is assumed to be 15% unless you prove more,
  • the remission (relief) program, renewed to April 8, 2027, gives eligible automakers a set number of surtax-free vehicles. It is not something a private importer can claim.

In practice that means a US-built car can owe a surtax of roughly 21% of its value before GST. Trade measures between the two countries have changed several times since 2025, so check the CBSA's current customs notices in the week you buy.

Step 4: The RIV inspection within 45 days

Once the car is in Canada, a vehicle in the RIV program needs a federal RIV inspection within 45 days of the declaration, at an authorized inspection centre. RIV lists the locations. The inspection checks that the car was modified, where needed, to meet Canadian standards and that recalls are cleared.

Don't let the deadline slide. RIV warns that failing to pass within the required time leads to non-compliance, which can be enforced with fines, penalties and removal of the vehicle under the Motor Vehicle Safety Act. Book the inspection as soon as your RIV fee is paid and any modifications are done.

Step 5: Provincial safety and registration

Registration is provincial, so the details vary. In Ontario, for a car from outside Canada, you register in person at a ServiceOntario centre and bring:

  • the Vehicle Import Form 1, with both stamps showing it met federal requirements,
  • the original US title,
  • a Safety Standards Certificate from a licensed inspection station. You can register without one, but you can't put plates on the car until you have it,
  • proof of Ontario insurance.

Ontario also collects the 8% provincial portion of the HST at ServiceOntario when the CBSA has already charged the 5% GST. Other provinces have their own inspection and tax rules, so check before the car arrives.

Is it still worth importing a car from the US?

For a car built in Canada or Mexico, or a vehicle over 15 years old, the math can still work. For a recent US-built car, add the 25% surtax, GST, provincial tax, the RIV fee, any modifications and your travel, then compare that total with the same car for sale in Canada. Price it against Canadian listings, not against the US sticker.

Common questions

How much does it cost to import a car from the US to Canada?

For most vehicles under 15 years old you pay the $325 RIV fee plus tax, 5% GST at the border, a $100 excise tax if the car has air conditioning, and provincial sales tax at registration. Depending on the car you may also owe 6.1% duty, the 25% counter-tariff on US vehicles and the green levy of $1,000 to $4,000 for fuel-inefficient models. Shipping, export agent, modification and inspection costs are extra.

Do I pay duty on a car bought in the US?

Not if it qualifies as originating under CUSMA, which generally covers vehicles built in Canada, the US or Mexico. A car built elsewhere, such as a German- or Japanese-built model bought in the US, pays 6.1% duty. Duty is separate from the 25% counter-tariff on US vehicles.

Is there a tariff on US cars coming into Canada in 2026?

Yes, as of the latest federal notices. Since April 9, 2025, Canada has applied a 25% counter-tariff on passenger vehicles and light trucks from the US. For CUSMA-compliant vehicles it is charged on the value minus Canadian and Mexican content, assumed to be 15% unless you prove more. The relief program is for automakers, not private importers, so check with the CBSA before you buy.

Can I drive a car from the US to Canada myself?

Yes. Many people drive the car to the border themselves. You still need to submit the export documents to US Customs and Border Protection at least 72 hours before you cross, present the car at the US export office, then declare it to the CBSA on the Canadian side. Make sure it is insured and legally plated for the drive.

What happens if I miss the 45-day RIV inspection?

RIV warns that failing to pass the federal inspection within the required time leads to non-compliance, which can be enforced with fines, penalties and removal of the vehicle under the Motor Vehicle Safety Act. Without the completed federal paperwork, your province will not register the car either.

Can I import a car that is more than 15 years old?

Yes. Vehicles manufactured more than 15 years ago, counted by month and year of manufacture, are not regulated for import under the Motor Vehicle Safety Act, so they skip the RIV program. You still follow the US export rules, pay any duty and taxes that apply, and pass your province's safety inspection.
