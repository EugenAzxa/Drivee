What’s in a vehicle history report? How to read one before you buy

A report shows what was recorded, not everything that happened

A vehicle history report is only as complete as the records behind it. Damage that was repaired for cash, never claimed and never reported will not appear. Read the report for what it says, then have the car inspected for what it can’t say.

Drivee Editorial Updated November 15, 2026 8 min read

A vehicle history report collects what has been recorded about a car under its 17-character VIN: insurance claims, odometer readings, registrations, title brands, theft reports and sometimes service visits. In both Canada and the United States it is one of the cheapest ways to catch a bad used car before you pay. Here is what a vehicle history report shows, how to read it line by line, and which official checks fill the gaps.

What is a vehicle history report?

It is a document built by matching a VIN against records from many sources: provincial and state registration and titling agencies, insurers, salvage yards and auctions, police theft databases, and in some cases repair shops and dealers. No single source sees a car’s whole life, so the report stitches the pieces into one timeline.

There are two broad kinds. Government records, such as Ontario’s Used Vehicle Information Package (UVIP) or a report through the US National Motor Vehicle Title Information System (NMVTIS), are narrow but official. Commercial reports pull from more sources, including accident and damage claims, and cost more. Most careful buyers end up using both.

What does a vehicle history report show?

Coverage varies by provider and by where the car has lived, but a full report usually covers these areas.

Accidents and damage claims

Insurance claims and police-reported collisions, often with a date, the area of the car that was hit and sometimes a claim amount. A claim amount helps you separate a scraped bumper from structural damage.

Odometer readings

Readings captured at registrations, title transfers, inspections, emissions tests and service visits. One reading on its own tells you little. A series of them, with dates, lets you check that the mileage only ever goes up and climbs at a believable pace.

Registration and title history

Where the car was registered and when, across provinces and US states. Cross-border history matters: a car that spent three years in a flood-prone state, or came into Canada from the US, deserves a closer look.

Title brands

A brand is a permanent mark on the record after a serious event. Ontario uses four: None, Salvage, Rebuilt and Irreparable. Only None and Rebuilt vehicles can be driven on Ontario roads, and a salvage car becomes Rebuilt only after passing a structural inspection. US states use their own terms, such as salvage, rebuilt, junk or flood, and the federal NMVTIS program notes that brand standards differ between jurisdictions.

Brands follow the car across borders

When a car from another province or a US state is registered in Ontario, its existing brand carries over. A vehicle branded Rebuilt elsewhere is registered as Salvage in Ontario until it passes an Ontario structural inspection.

Theft records

Whether the VIN has been reported stolen, and sometimes whether it was later recovered.

Liens

Whether a lender has registered a security interest against the car. In Canada liens are registered provincially, so a lien check is tied to a province. If you buy a car with a lien on it, the lender may still have a claim on it after the sale.

Service records

Oil changes, inspections and repairs that shops chose to report. These are patchy. Plenty of good independent garages report nothing.

How do you read a vehicle history report?

Work through it in this order, with the car or the ad in front of you.

  1. Confirm it’s the right car. Match the VIN on the report to the VIN on the dashboard plate, the door-jamb label and the ownership or title. Check the year, make, model, engine and trim against the ad.
  2. Check the brand first. A Salvage, Irreparable, junk or flood brand changes everything that follows. A Rebuilt brand is not automatically a no, but it should lower the price and raise the bar for the inspection.
  3. Lay out the timeline. List each registration and its location. Note the gaps. A car that vanished from the records for two years and reappeared somewhere else needs an explanation.
  4. Plot the odometer readings. Each reading should be higher than the one before it. A lower reading later in the timeline is the clearest sign of a problem. Our guide to spotting odometer rollback covers this step in detail.
  5. Read every damage entry. Note the date, the impact area and any amount. Then look at that part of the car in person: paint that doesn’t match, uneven panel gaps, overspray on rubber seals.
  6. Check theft and lien status last, and again on the day you pay. Both can change between your first check and the sale.

Tip: watch the units. Canadian records are usually in kilometres and US records in miles. A US car with 60,000 miles has about 96,500 km on it. Mixing the two can make a normal car look rolled back, or hide one that is.

What are the red flags on a vehicle history report?

  • An odometer reading that goes down, or barely moves over several years while the car keeps being registered and serviced.
  • A total-loss record, salvage auction listing or brand the seller didn’t mention.
  • A recent move between provinces or states shortly before the sale, especially after a damage claim. Moving a car around is sometimes used to obscure its past.
  • Several owners in a short time.
  • Damage claims in the same area more than once, or a large claim on a car advertised as “accident free”.
  • A lien registered against the car while the seller says it is paid off.
  • A VIN, year or engine on the report that doesn’t match the car in front of you.

One red flag isn’t always a deal-breaker. A minor claim from years ago on an otherwise clean history is normal. What matters is whether the story the seller tells matches the record.

What can’t a vehicle history report tell you?

  • Unreported repairs. If an owner paid a body shop in cash and no claim was made, there is usually no record.
  • Current condition. Brakes, tires, rust, leaks, engine and transmission health don’t appear on a history report.
  • Gaps between sources. Providers rely on what their sources report. Records can arrive late, and some insurers, shops or jurisdictions share more than others.
  • Exact repair quality. A report may show that a car was repaired, not whether it was repaired well.

Treat a clean report as “nothing bad was recorded”, not as “nothing bad happened”.

What official checks can you run in Canada and the US?

Several government and industry tools are free or low cost. They don’t replace a full report, but they are authoritative for what they cover.

Canada

  • Ontario UVIP ($20). From ServiceOntario. It lists vehicle details, Ontario registration history with each owner’s odometer reading, lien information, the average wholesale value, the last known status, whether the car was reported as wrecked, and its brand. A private seller in Ontario is legally required to give the buyer one, with some exceptions, including sales by registered dealers. More detail is in our free VIN check guide.
  • Alberta Vehicle Information Report. From a registry agent or online. It shows the vehicle’s status (such as active, rebuilt or salvage), the odometer reading if one was provided, registration dates and locations, and the number of liens registered in Alberta. It covers Alberta records only.
  • ICBC vehicle claims history (British Columbia, $20 plus GST). Shows damage claimed through ICBC, the vehicle’s status, whether it was imported to BC and possible stolen reports. It covers BC claims only.
  • CPIC stolen vehicle search (free). The Canadian Police Information Centre website, run by the RCMP, lets anyone search a VIN for police stolen-vehicle reports.
  • Transport Canada recalls (free). The Motor Vehicle Safety Recalls Database lists recalls for Canadian-market vehicles.

United States

  • NMVTIS report. Sold through approved providers listed on the official NMVTIS site. It is deliberately short: current title and brand history, the latest reported odometer reading, any insurer total-loss or salvage determination, and any transfer to a recycler, junk or salvage yard. Prices are set by each provider.
  • NICB VINCheck (free). The National Insurance Crime Bureau checks a VIN against theft and total-loss records from participating insurers. It is limited to five searches per IP address in a 24-hour period.
  • NHTSA recall lookup (free). Enter a VIN to see open safety recalls on US-market vehicles.

Tip: official checks are usually limited to one jurisdiction. A Canadian record won’t show a US flood brand, and a state record won’t show an Ontario lien. A car that has crossed the border is where a full cross-border report earns its cost.

Is a clean vehicle history report enough?

No. The report tells you about the past. An inspection tells you about the car you’re about to own. Use them in this order: free checks to rule out obvious problems, a full report on the one or two cars you’re serious about, then a pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic who works for you, not the seller. If the inspection finds repaired damage the report doesn’t mention, that is a reason to ask questions, renegotiate or walk away.

Common questions

What is in a vehicle history report?

Most full vehicle history reports cover accident and damage claims, odometer readings over time, registration and title history across provinces and states, title brands such as salvage or rebuilt, theft records, liens and some service records. Exact coverage depends on the provider and on where the car has been registered.

Does a vehicle history report show all accidents?

No. It shows accidents that were reported to its sources, usually through insurance claims or police reports. Damage repaired privately without a claim often leaves no record, which is why a pre-purchase inspection is still important.

Is a vehicle history report the same as Ontario’s UVIP?

No. The UVIP is a $20 government package from ServiceOntario covering Ontario registration history, odometer readings, liens, status and brand. A commercial history report adds sources such as accident claims and US records. Many Ontario buyers use both.

Is there a free vehicle history report?

Free tools cover parts of the history. In the US, NICB VINCheck shows theft and total-loss records from participating insurers and NHTSA shows open recalls. In Canada, the CPIC site checks police stolen-vehicle reports and Transport Canada lists recalls. Accident history and a full timeline usually need a paid report.

Does a history report work for a car that came from the US?

It depends on the provider. Reports that cover both countries can show US title brands and odometer readings alongside Canadian records. Government checks are usually limited to one province or state, so a cross-border car needs a report that covers both.

What is the biggest red flag on a vehicle history report?

An odometer reading that is lower than an earlier one, and a salvage, flood or total-loss record the seller did not disclose. Either one is a reason to stop and ask questions before going further.
