Used car scams in Canada and the US: curbsiders, VIN cloning, fake deposits

Two rules stop most scams

Never send money for a car you haven't seen in person. And never pay until the VIN on the car, the seller's ID and the ownership or title all match. Almost every scam below breaks one of those rules.

Drivee Editorial Updated November 15, 2026 9 min read

Used car scams in Canada and the US mostly follow a handful of patterns: a dealer pretending to be a private seller, a stolen car wearing another car's identity, a title cleaned of its history, an odometer turned back, or a listing for a car that doesn't exist. Each has tells. Here is how they work on Facebook Marketplace, Kijiji and Craigslist, and what to do if one reaches you.

What is a curbsider?

OMVIC, Ontario's dealer regulator, defines curbsiders as illegal, unlicensed vehicle dealers. They usually pose as private sellers, and some work out of small automotive businesses such as repair shops. In the US the same practice is called curbstoning. Every state has its own dealer licensing rules, and someone who keeps buying and reselling cars for profit without a licence is breaking them.

The cars are the real problem. OMVIC says many curbsider vehicles are previous write-offs with undisclosed accident repairs, have tampered odometers, or both. The seller is often hard or impossible to find after the sale.

OMVIC's warning signs:

  • the seller has several vehicles for sale,
  • the price is below market value,
  • the car isn't registered in the seller's name, or was registered to them only recently,
  • the "private" seller appears to operate from a business, or the car wears a yellow mechanic's plate or a dealer plate,
  • the seller discourages a history report or the UVIP, or refuses an inspection by your mechanic,
  • the seller won't give a receipt showing their name and address.
You lose your protections

OMVIC is explicit: people who buy privately from a curbsider are not protected by OMVIC, its compensation fund or the Motor Vehicle Dealers Act. Buying from a registered dealer is what brings those protections.

What is VIN cloning, and how do you spot a cloned car?

VIN cloning gives a stolen or salvaged car the identity of a legitimate, registered one. Thieves copy a real VIN from a similar car, put it on fake plates and labels, and pair it with forged or fraudulently obtained paperwork. A history report on that VIN will look clean, because it describes the other car.

How to catch it:

  • Read the VIN in every place you can find it: the windshield plate, the driver's door-jamb label and any other labels. They must match each other and the ownership or title, character for character.
  • Look hard at the windshield plate. Scratches, glue, loose or non-factory rivets are reasons to walk away.
  • Compare the report with the car. Wrong colour, trim, engine or options can mean the VIN belongs to a different vehicle.
  • Run the free NICB VINCheck. It looks for theft and salvage records reported by participating US insurers.

What is title washing, and can paperwork be faked?

Title washing hides a car's past by re-registering it somewhere with different branding rules, so a salvage or flood brand drops off the new title. The U.S. Department of Justice notes that brand rules vary by state, and its NMVTIS database, reached through vehiclehistory.gov, exists partly to stop this by pulling title and brand history from state agencies, insurers and salvage yards. Our guide to spotting a flood-damaged car covers how this plays out after storms.

Paperwork itself can be forged or edited. In Ontario, don't rely on a UVIP the seller prints or texts you. You can order your own for $20 from ServiceOntario with the VIN or plate. In the US, ask your state motor vehicle agency how to verify a title, and run an NMVTIS report.

How common is odometer fraud?

Common enough to check every time. NHTSA estimates that more than 450,000 vehicles are sold each year in the US with false odometer readings. In Ontario, OMVIC warns that many curbsider cars have had their odometers tampered with. Digital odometers can be altered too.

What helps:

  • Odometer readings in the UVIP, title records or a history report should only go up over time. A drop, or years with no readings at all, needs an explanation.
  • Wear should match the distance. A worn steering wheel, shiny pedals and a sagging driver's seat don't fit a car with very low mileage.
  • Service records, oil-change stickers and inspection reports often carry a reading and a date.

How do fake deposit, escrow and shipping scams work?

These scams sell a car that the "seller" doesn't have. The FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center describes the pattern: an ad for a car priced below market, a seller who only communicates by text or email, and a story about why the car can't be seen. The seller is relocating, deployed with the military, going through a divorce, or selling a late relative's car.

Then comes the payment request:

  • A deposit to "hold" the car, sent by e-transfer, wire or a payment app, before you've seen it.
  • Fake escrow. You're told to pay an "independent" company that will hold the money and ship the car. The FBI says no vehicle is ever delivered, and the escrow company may not exist.
  • A fake marketplace guarantee. Scammers claim the deal is protected by a real company's buyer program. The FBI notes, for example, that eBay's vehicle purchase protection only covers transactions that start and finish on eBay Motors.
  • Gift cards for the sale price, with the codes sent to the seller.

The Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre warns about the same tactics on classified and resale sites, including fake ads and fake company websites, and gives the same advice: a price that seems too good to be true usually is.

How do you avoid buying a stolen car?

Canada has had a serious auto theft problem. The federal government's National Action Plan on Combatting Auto Theft cites an Insurance Bureau of Canada estimate of a record $1.2 billion in theft claims paid in 2022, and says about 75% of vehicles recovered from sea containers in Montreal had been stolen in Ontario. In the US, NICB counted 659,880 vehicle thefts in 2025, down 23% from 2024. Fewer thefts is good news, but some stolen cars still get resold to buyers who had no idea.

Ontario's advice is useful anywhere: stolen vehicles often have errors in their histories, such as the wrong colour or odometer readings. Check that:

  • the seller's government ID matches the name on the ownership or title,
  • the seller has both keys, and a reasonable answer if not,
  • you meet at the seller's home address shown on the paperwork, not only in a parking lot,
  • the VIN checks above all pass.

If police later identify a car you bought as stolen, you can lose the car and still be out the money.

How to avoid used car scams: a short checklist

  1. Check the VIN from the ad before you travel.
  2. See the car in person, in daylight, and test drive it.
  3. Match everything: VIN on the car, ownership or title, seller ID, history report.
  4. Get an independent inspection by a mechanic who works for you.
  5. Pay safely. Meet at a bank and use a traceable method such as a bank draft issued in front of the seller. If you're the one accepting a draft or certified cheque, confirm it with the issuing branch using a number you look up yourself. Never pay by wire to a stranger, gift cards or cryptocurrency.
  6. Leave with the signed paperwork, and register the car promptly. Our Ontario buying checklist covers the documents in detail.

Tip: some police services offer marked exchange zones at their stations. A seller who won't meet in a public, well-lit place, or at a bank, is telling you something.

Where do you report a used car scam?

Report even if you didn't lose money. It helps investigators connect cases.

  • Canada: your local police first, then the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre at reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca or 1-888-495-8501 (Monday to Friday, 10 am to 4:45 pm Eastern).
  • Ontario curbsiders and dealers: OMVIC's online complaint form, or 416-226-4500 / 1-800-943-6002.
  • US: your local police, the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, and the FBI's IC3 at ic3.gov for online scams, whatever the amount. Dealer complaints go to your state attorney general.
  • Suspected stolen or cloned cars: police, and in the US, the NICB.
  • The platform: report the listing to Marketplace, Kijiji or Craigslist so it comes down.

Common questions

How do I know if a private seller is a curbsider?

Warning signs include several cars for sale at once, a price below market, a car not registered in the seller's name or only recently registered to them, a dealer or mechanic plate on the car, and a seller who discourages a history report or an independent inspection.

Is it safe to pay a deposit on a car I haven't seen?

No. Paying before you have seen the car in person and checked its paperwork is how most fake listing scams work. A seller who insists on a deposit by wire, gift card or cryptocurrency, or who says the car must be shipped, is a red flag.

How can I tell if a VIN has been cloned?

Compare the VIN in every place it appears on the car, including the windshield plate and the door-jamb label, with the ownership or title and the history report. Look for scratched, loose or re-riveted plates, and details in the report such as colour or trim that don't match the car.

What is the safest way to pay a private seller for a car?

Meet at a bank, check the seller's ID against the ownership or title, and pay with a traceable method such as a bank draft issued in front of the seller. Never pay by wire transfer to a stranger, gift cards or cryptocurrency, and never pay before you have the signed paperwork.

Where do I report a car scam in Canada?

Report it to your local police and to the Canadian Anti-Fraud Centre online at reportcyberandfraud.canada.ca or by phone at 1-888-495-8501. In Ontario, report a curbsider or a dealer problem to OMVIC.

Where do I report a car scam in the US?

Report it to your local police, to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov and, for online scams, to the FBI's Internet Crime Complaint Center at ic3.gov. Your state attorney general handles dealer complaints, and the NICB takes reports of suspected stolen or cloned vehicles.
