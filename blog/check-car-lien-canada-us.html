How to check if a car has a lien (Canada and US) before you buy

A lien follows the car, not the seller

If you buy a car with money still owing on it, the debt does not leave with the seller. Ontario warns that once the transfer is complete, a lien claimant has a right to the car or the amount of the lien from the buyer. Check before you pay, and check again on the day.

What is a lien on a car?

A lien is a lender's legal claim on a vehicle until a debt is paid. Ontario describes it as a form of security put on a vehicle to make sure its owner pays any outstanding debt. The most common case is an ordinary car loan: the bank or finance company lends the money and keeps a claim on the car until the last payment.

A lien does not stop the owner from driving the car, and it does not stop them from advertising it. That is why it catches buyers out. The seller hands over keys and paperwork that look normal, and the lender's claim stays attached to the car.

Leased cars are a related trap. A leased car belongs to the leasing company, not the person driving it, so the "seller" may not own what they are selling.

How do I check if a car has a lien in the US?

In the US, the certificate of title is the main record. Each state runs its own titling system, but the pattern is similar.

  • Read the title. When a car is financed, the lender is normally recorded as lienholder on the title. If a lienholder is listed, ask for proof it has been released.
  • Watch for electronic titles. Many states run electronic lien and title (ELT) programs. While the loan is open, the lender holds an electronic title record instead of a paper certificate, and the state prints a clean paper title only after the lender releases the lien electronically. If the seller says the title is "with the bank," that usually means there is still a loan.
  • Ask the state DMV. Some states offer a title or lien status lookup online or at a DMV office. Check the DMV for the state where the car is titled, which may not be where it is parked.

Don't count on a general vehicle history report for this. NMVTIS reports, for example, are built around title brands, odometer readings and total-loss records, and the federal government describes them as intentionally concise. Loan balances are not one of their core indicators.

How do I check for a lien in Canada?

Canada has no title certificate, so a car's ownership papers won't show a lender. Liens are registered in each province's personal property registry and searched by VIN or by the owner's name.

Ontario. The Used Vehicle Information Package includes lien information, and a private seller is legally required to give you one. You can also run your own search of the Personal Property Security Registration system through ServiceOntario's Access Now service. An online search costs $8. Run it against the VIN on the car itself, not the one in the ad. Our free VIN check guide covers what else the UVIP shows.

British Columbia. A serial number search of the Personal Property Registry using the VIN is the usual check for a used car. A search you run yourself costs $7 plus a $1.50 operator fee and GST on that fee. A search done by government staff costs $10.

Alberta. Any registry agent office can run a personal property search by serial number, which covers motor vehicles. The Vehicle Information Report sold by registry agents shows the number of liens registered, and a full personal property search gives the details.

Quebec. The SAAQ tells used-car buyers to check the RDPRM before buying to make sure the vehicle is free of debt. You can search by the 17-character VIN online or by phone, and the fee depends on how and what you search.

Each registry only covers its own province

An Ontario search won't show a loan registered in Alberta. If the car was recently registered in another province, search that province's registry too.

What happens if you buy a car with a lien?

In Canada. The lender's claim generally stays with the car. ServiceOntario puts it plainly: once the transfer is complete, a lien claimant has a right to the car or the amount of the lien from the buyer. In practice that can mean paying off someone else's loan to keep the car, or losing it to repossession and chasing the seller for your money.

In the US. A lien recorded on the title also survives a private sale. The state generally won't issue you a clean title in your name until the lienholder releases it, and the lender can still repossess if the loan isn't paid. There is a narrower protection when you buy from a dealer: under the Uniform Commercial Code, a buyer in the ordinary course of business takes free of a security interest created by the seller, such as the dealer's own inventory financing. That rule does not automatically clear a previous owner's car loan, so a dealer still has to pay that off.

How do I buy a car that still has a loan on it?

A lien is not a deal-breaker. Plenty of good cars are sold before the loan is finished. The goal is simple: the lender gets paid first, and you get proof.

  1. Get a payout letter. Ask the seller to get a written payout statement from the lender showing the exact balance, the account it applies to and how long the figure is good for.
  2. Pay the lender directly. Pay the payout amount straight to the lender, by bank draft or wire, never to the seller. If the price is higher than the loan, the seller gets the difference separately. Doing the deal at the lender's branch, with the seller present, is the cleanest version.
  3. Get the release in writing. Ask the lender for a letter confirming the loan is paid. In Canada, the lender should register a discharge of its registration. Ontario advises buyers to have the seller get that discharge registered before the sale is completed. In the US, the lender signs the release on a paper title or sends an electronic release to the state.
  4. Search again. Run the registry search or title check again on the day you pay. A search is a snapshot, and a new lien can be registered after your first check.
  5. Keep everything. Bill of sale, payout letter, proof of payment and the discharge or release.

Is it safer to buy from a dealer than a private seller?

On liens, usually yes, because a licensed dealer has the paperwork process and the regulator behind it. In Ontario, buyers who dealt with an OMVIC-registered dealer can apply to OMVIC's Motor Vehicle Dealers Compensation Fund in specific situations, such as a dealer that goes bankrupt or a court judgment the dealer won't pay, within two years of the issue. People who buy privately have no access to the fund. In the US, dealer protections are set state by state, so check the dealer is licensed with your state.

If you trade in a car with a loan on it, the dealer normally pays that loan off. Call your lender a week or so later to confirm it actually happened.

Red flags that suggest money is still owed

  • The seller's name doesn't match the title or the ownership document.
  • The title is "lost," just reissued, or "with the bank, but it's paid off."
  • The seller won't let you call the lender or come to the branch.
  • They want cash, a quick sale, or payment before you can run a search.
  • The price is well under market for no clear reason.
  • The car was registered in another province or state very recently.
  • The seller can't produce a UVIP in an Ontario private sale, though the law requires it.

Common questions

How can I check if a car has a lien for free?

In the US, reading the title is free: a financed car normally lists the lender as lienholder. In Canada, official searches cost a small fee. An Ontario PPSR search is $8 online, and a self-service BC Personal Property Registry search is $7 plus a $1.50 operator fee. Ontario private sellers must also give the buyer a UVIP, which includes lien information.

Can you buy a car that has a lien on it?

Yes, if the loan is paid off as part of the sale. Get a payout letter from the lender, pay the lender directly, and get written confirmation that the loan is paid and the lien is discharged or released before you take the car.

What happens if I buy a car with a lien by mistake?

The lender's claim generally stays with the car. In Ontario, a lien claimant has a right to the car or the amount of the lien from the buyer once the transfer is complete. In the US, you may not be able to get a clean title until the lien is released, and the lender can still repossess.

How do I check for a lien on a car in Ontario?

Ask the seller for the Used Vehicle Information Package, which includes lien information, or search the Personal Property Security Registration system through ServiceOntario using the VIN. An online search costs $8. Search again close to the day you pay.

Does a vehicle history report show liens?

Not always. Many history reports focus on accidents, brands, odometer and ownership, and sell lien checks separately for specific provinces. Drivee's $30 plan includes an Ontario lien check. For other provinces or US states, use the provincial registry or the state title record.

What is a lien release letter?

It is a letter from the lender confirming the loan is paid in full and that it is releasing its claim on the vehicle. In Canada the lender should also register a discharge in the provincial registry. In the US the lender signs off on the paper title or sends an electronic release to the state.
