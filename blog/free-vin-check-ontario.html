How to check a used car’s VIN in Ontario for free, and what it won’t tell you

Free covers the specs, not the history

A free VIN check confirms what the car is and whether it has open recalls. It does not show who owned it, whether money is owing on it, or whether it was written off. In Ontario those answers come from the $20 UVIP, which a private seller is legally required to give you.

Where to find the VIN

The vehicle identification number is 17 characters long. It uses digits and capital letters, but never the letters I, O or Q, so they can’t be mistaken for 1 and 0.

On most cars you’ll find it in these places:

  • on a small plate at the base of the windshield on the driver’s side, readable from outside,
  • on the label in the driver’s door jamb,
  • on the vehicle permit (the ownership) and the insurance slip.

Tip: photograph the VIN on the car itself, not just the one in the ad or on the paperwork. Ontario tells buyers to confirm the VIN on the vehicle matches the permit. If the two don’t match, stop there.

What a free VIN decode tells you

A VIN is structured. The first three characters identify the manufacturer, the 10th character encodes the model year, and the 9th is a check digit that catches typos. A decoder reads those positions against data the manufacturer reports.

The most common free source is NHTSA’s vPIC decoder, which most manufacturers selling in North America report to. A decode typically confirms:

  • model year, make, model and often the trim,
  • body style, engine and drivetrain,
  • the plant where it was built.

That sounds basic, but it catches real problems. If the ad says “AWD, V6” and the VIN says front-wheel drive with a four-cylinder, you’ve learned something important for free.

Checking for open recalls

Recalls are free to check, and worth it. Transport Canada keeps a Motor Vehicle Safety Recalls Database you can search by make, model and year. Since January 1, 2025, federal rules also require manufacturers to publish recall information online, and designated manufacturers must offer a VIN lookup on their own websites.

A make-model-year result tells you a recall exists for that population of cars. The manufacturer’s VIN lookup (or a dealer for that brand) tells you whether this car still needs the repair.

Canadian vs. U.S. recalls

U.S. recall tools cover U.S.-market vehicles. A car built for Canada can be covered by a recall under a different number, or not at all. For a Canadian car, check Transport Canada or the manufacturer’s Canadian site too.

Tip: recall repairs are done by the manufacturer’s dealers. If a recall is open, ask the seller to get it done before you buy, or book it yourself right after.

What a free check doesn’t tell you

A decode only reads what is built into the number. It can’t tell you:

  • how many owners the car has had, or where it was registered,
  • whether the odometer reading is consistent over time,
  • whether it was written off and rebuilt,
  • whether a lender still has a claim on it,
  • whether it was in a collision that never led to a write-off,
  • its mechanical condition today.

Each of those has its own source, covered below.

The Ontario UVIP: $20, and the seller owes you one

The Used Vehicle Information Package comes from ServiceOntario and costs $20. In a private sale, the seller is legally required to give one to the buyer. Purchases from a registered dealer and transfers between family members are among the exceptions.

According to ServiceOntario, the UVIP includes:

  • vehicle details: year, make, model, colour, body type, cylinders,
  • Ontario registration history: current and past owners, their city, and odometer readings,
  • lien information,
  • the average wholesale value, which sets the minimum value for the retail sales tax you’ll pay,
  • its last known status, including whether it is registered as unfit,
  • whether it has been reported as wrecked, and its brand,
  • a bill of sale section.

The brand matters. Under Ontario’s branding program a written-off car is branded Salvage (can be repaired) or Irreparable (parts or scrap only, never driven again in Ontario). A repaired salvage car that passes a structural inspection becomes Rebuilt. Only “None” and “Rebuilt” vehicles can be driven on Ontario roads.

Tip: you don’t have to wait for the seller. You can order a UVIP yourself with the VIN or plate, online (mailed, up to about 10 days) or same day at any ServiceOntario centre.

Checking for liens

A lien means someone, usually a lender, has a security interest in the car. If you buy a car with a lien on it, the lender may still have a claim against it after the sale. The UVIP shows lien information, and you can also search Ontario’s Personal Property Security Registration system through ServiceOntario for a small fee.

If a lien shows up, it isn’t automatically a deal-breaker, but the debt needs to be paid off and the lien discharged before your money changes hands. The simplest way is to pay the lender directly, or complete the sale at the lender’s branch.

Check close to the day you pay

A lien search is a snapshot. If weeks pass between your search and the sale, run it again.

Paid history reports (CARFAX Canada and others)

Accident and damage history is the main thing you pay for. CARFAX Canada, the best-known provider, sells reports built from data its sources report, including accident and damage records, Canadian and U.S. registration and branding, and stolen-vehicle status. Its lien check is sold as a separate option, and its own reports note that they only contain what participating sources reported.

Drivee also offers a paid full history report from the same report page, with an Ontario lien check as an option. Whichever you use, read what’s included before you buy.

Tip: a clean report means nothing bad was reported. Cash repairs never show up. That’s why the last step is always an inspection.

A sensible order to do it in

  1. Free decode and recall check from the VIN in the ad. Rule out cars that aren’t what they claim to be.
  2. Ask the seller for the UVIP. Check the owners, odometer history, brand and liens.
  3. Paid history report on the one or two cars you’re serious about.
  4. Pre-purchase inspection by a mechanic who works for you, not the seller.

Common questions

Can I check a car’s history in Ontario for free?

Partly. A free VIN decode confirms the year, make, model and factory specs, and free recall tools show safety recalls. Ontario ownership history, odometer readings, brands and liens come from the Used Vehicle Information Package, which costs $20 from ServiceOntario. Accident and damage claims usually need a paid history report.

Does a free VIN check show accidents?

No. A decode only reads what is encoded in the VIN itself. Accident and damage history comes from insurance and repair data, which is sold in paid reports. A total-loss write-off shows up as a Salvage, Rebuilt or Irreparable brand on the UVIP.

Who pays for the UVIP in a private sale?

In a private sale the seller is legally required to give the buyer a UVIP. A buyer can also order one for $20 before the sale, online or same-day at a ServiceOntario centre.

How do I check if a used car has a lien in Ontario?

The UVIP includes lien information. You can also search the Personal Property Security Registration system through ServiceOntario. Check again close to the day you pay, because a new lien can be registered after your first check.
