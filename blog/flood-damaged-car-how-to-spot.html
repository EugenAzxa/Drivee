How to spot a flood-damaged car before you buy (Canada and US)

A clean title is not a clean car

Flood brands usually depend on an insurer writing the car off. A car that was never claimed, or that was re-registered somewhere with different rules, can arrive with no brand at all. Run the free checks, then trust what you can see, smell and scan.

Drivee Editorial Updated November 15, 2026 8 min read

A flood-damaged car can look spotless on a lot or in a Marketplace photo. After a hurricane, a spring flood or a storm that fills underground garages, damaged cars need somewhere to go, and some get dried, detailed and listed for sale far from where they flooded, sometimes in another state or across the border in Canada. Here is how to spot one before you pay, which records to check on each side of the border, and what to do if you already bought one.

Why do flood-damaged cars end up on the used market?

When an insured car floods, the insurer usually writes it off and sells it through a salvage auction. The state or province is then supposed to brand its title so every later buyer knows. Many of those cars are parted out. Some are repaired, legally or otherwise, and sold again.

The trouble starts when the history gets lost. The U.S. Federal Trade Commission warns that storm-damaged cars are sometimes cleaned up and taken out of state for sale. Each state sets its own brands, and the U.S. Department of Justice notes that a car treated as salvage in one place may not carry that brand in another. Moving a car to a state with looser rules and re-titling it there is called title washing.

There is also a simpler gap. If the owner had no coverage for the damage, no insurer ever wrote the car off, and no brand was ever applied. That car can look clean on every database.

Why it matters: the FTC notes that water can slowly destroy a car's mechanical and electrical systems and cause rust and corrosion. A flood car can pass a test drive and fail months later, including in safety systems like airbags and ABS that rely on sensors and wiring.

What are the signs of a flood-damaged car?

Bring a flashlight, see the car in daylight, and give yourself time. No single sign proves a flood, but several together should end the deal.

  • Smell. A musty, mildew smell, or a heavy perfume of air freshener or cleaning products trying to cover one. Check the trunk too. Close the doors and sit inside for a minute with the heat on.
  • Silt and debris. Fine mud or sand under the seats, under the dash, in the glovebox, in the spare-tire well and under the trunk floor. Pull a seat belt all the way out: water stains or grit near the end of the webbing are hard to fake away.
  • Rust in odd places. Rust on seat rails and bolts, pedal brackets, screws under the dash or door hinges on a car that is otherwise in good shape. Interior metal should not rust.
  • Carpet and upholstery. Carpet that is loose, stained, damp or newer than the rest of the interior. Lift a corner near a door if you can.
  • Lights and glass. Condensation, fogging or a waterline inside headlights and taillights.
  • Electrical faults. Test every window, lock, seat motor, mirror, gauge, the radio and screen, the heater and air conditioning. Watch for warning lights that come on and stay on, or that never light up at start-up at all.
  • Engine bay and underbody. A line of dried debris on the firewall or radiator, mud in crevices, or corrosion on wiring connectors.
Watch the story too

A low price, a car recently brought in from a storm-hit region, a seller who won't let you look under the carpet or book an inspection: none of these prove a flood, but they raise the bar for everything else.

Does a flood car always have a flood title in the US?

No. Use the free and low-cost checks, but read their limits.

NICB VINCheck. The National Insurance Crime Bureau runs a free lookup at nicb.org, limited to five searches per IP address in 24 hours. It checks a VIN against theft, salvage and flood records reported by participating member insurers. The FTC points out it only helps if the car was insured when the damage happened.

NMVTIS. The National Motor Vehicle Title Information System, reached through vehiclehistory.gov, collects data from state titling agencies, insurers, auto recyclers, and junk and salvage yards. A report covers current title information and brand history (labels such as junk, salvage or flood), the latest odometer reading, and total-loss and salvage records. You buy it through approved private providers, who set their own prices. The Justice Department is clear that not every entity reports yet, so a clean result can be a false negative.

Tip: ask to see the actual title, and the previous one if the car was re-titled recently. A fresh title from a different state on a car that just came out of a flood zone deserves a closer look.

How do Canadian provinces brand flood-damaged cars?

Canada's provinces run their own branding programs, but the flood rule is shared. The Insurance Bureau of Canada says any vehicle flooded to the bottom of the dash must be branded non-repairable (also called irreparable), and that every Canadian jurisdiction follows this guideline. Such a vehicle can never be registered for use on a Canadian road.

In Ontario, a written-off car is branded Salvage or Irreparable, and only vehicles branded None or Rebuilt can be driven. A car brought in from another province or a U.S. state keeps its brand, except that a Rebuilt brand drops to Salvage until the car passes a structural inspection at a DriveON inspection centre. That carry-over only works if the brand existed in the first place.

Two free or cheap checks help in Canada:

  • IBC VIN Verify is a free search of vehicles reported as non-repairable in Alberta, Ontario and the Atlantic provinces. IBC notes it may not include every flooded or fire-damaged vehicle.
  • The Ontario UVIP ($20 from ServiceOntario, and a private seller must give you one) shows the car's brand and Ontario history. Our free VIN check guide explains what else it covers.

Will a vehicle history report show flood damage?

It may. The FTC says a history report may list accidents and flood damage, but typically won't list mechanical problems. Every report is limited to what its sources reported. A flood that never became an insurance claim, or a repair paid in cash, won't appear anywhere.

Use a report to catch the cars with a recorded past: a total loss, a brand, a gap in registration, or a sudden move from a storm-hit region. Then let the physical checks and the mechanic handle the rest.

Should you get a mechanic to check for water damage?

Yes, and tell them you're worried about a flood so they look in the right places. A good flood check goes past a test drive:

  • scanning every control module for stored fault codes, not just the engine,
  • pulling a kick panel or a carpet edge to look at wiring and connectors for green or white corrosion,
  • checking engine oil, transmission and differential fluid for a milky look that can mean water got in,
  • looking inside the air filter box and around the starter and lower engine for silt,
  • checking that the airbag and ABS lights work as they should.

Pay for an inspector who works for you, not the seller. If the seller refuses an inspection, treat that as your answer.

What if you already bought a flood car?

Stop driving it if warning lights for the airbags, brakes or stability control are on. Then document everything: photos of silt and rust, the mechanic's findings, the ad, messages and the bill of sale.

In Ontario, dealers registered with OMVIC must disclose in writing if a vehicle was branded or declared a total loss, and whether it was registered outside Ontario and where. If the contract did not disclose an irreparable, salvage or rebuilt brand, you can cancel within 90 days. OMVIC also notes the Consumer Protection Act can give further rights for misrepresentation for up to one year. Private sales are not covered by OMVIC, so your route there is usually small claims court.

In the US, dealers must post a Buyers Guide on every used car saying whether it is sold as is or with a warranty. The FTC suggests reporting a dealer who knowingly sold a storm-damaged or salvage car as sound to the NICB, to ReportFraud.ftc.gov and to your state attorney general.

Common questions

How can you tell if a used car has flood damage?

Use your nose and a flashlight. Look for a musty smell or heavy air freshener, silt or sand under the seats, in the spare-tire well and along the seat-belt webbing, rust on seat bolts and under the dash, fogged or water-lined lights, and electrical faults. Then run the VIN through the free NICB VINCheck and have a mechanic scan the car.

Will a VIN check show flood damage?

Sometimes. A VIN check shows flood damage only if it was reported, usually because an insurer wrote the car off or a state or province branded the title. A car that was never claimed, or whose brand was lost when it was re-registered elsewhere, can come back clean.

Is NICB VINCheck free?

Yes. The National Insurance Crime Bureau's VINCheck is free and limited to five searches per IP address in 24 hours. It looks for theft, salvage and flood records reported by participating insurers, so it only covers cars that were insured when the damage happened.

Can a flood-damaged car be registered in Canada?

It depends on how deep the water went and how the car was branded. The Insurance Bureau of Canada says a vehicle flooded to the bottom of the dash must be branded non-repairable, also called irreparable, and can never be registered for use on a Canadian road. In Ontario, only vehicles branded None or Rebuilt can be driven.

Can I return a flood-damaged car I bought from a dealer?

In Ontario, you can cancel a dealer purchase within 90 days if the contract did not disclose in writing that the car was branded irreparable, salvage or rebuilt. In the US, your rights depend on the Buyers Guide and state law, so contact your state attorney general. Private sales offer far less protection.

How long does flood damage take to show up?

It can take weeks or months. Water left in wiring, connectors and control modules keeps corroding after the car is dried and cleaned, so a flood car can drive normally on a test drive and develop electrical faults later. That is why a scan and a close inspection matter more than a short drive.
